AidGapHumanitarian funding monitor
What is this?

The funding gap is the difference between what humanitarian appeals ask for and what donors actually give.

AidGap compares the people in need with the money that arrives, crisis by crisis, and ranks the crises being left furthest behind.

04The ranking

Who is being left behind?

What the score means

The Neglect Score runs from 0 to 100. 100 means a crisis is the worst off of all of them on every measure; 0 means the best off. It is a ranking among these crises, not a measure of how bad things are in absolute terms.

Each crisis is lined up against all the others on three measures. Being near the bottom earns points:

How little of the appeal is funded (counts 40%)How few dollars reach each person in need (40%)How many people are affected (20%)

The weights are fixed so every crisis is judged the same way.

      09Method, limits and sources

      What this can and can't say.

      • Funding and need: funding per appeal from OCHA FTS; people in need from HDX HAPI (intersectoral, national level). Funding is for the current year.
      • The score is relative: 100 means the most neglected of the crises compared, not that anything is "solved" at 0. Weights are fixed at 40/40/20, and a robustness check re-ran the ranking under six other weightings.
      • Dollars per person is a blunt measure. Aid costs far more per person in some settings (for example a flash appeal after a disaster) than in a long-running crisis, so crises with high costs per person look better funded on this measure than they may be.
      • Regional response plans (refugee-hosting countries) are excluded because per-country funding is unreliable there. Where a country has its own plan plus a slice of a regional one, only its own plan is used.
      • Older or hand-found needs figures are flagged: dollars per person mixes two years or two sources, so the rank is indicative.
      • Donor totals count money given to one country alone and all flows FTS tracks, including outside the formal appeal, so they are larger than the appeal funding. Money split across several countries is left out to avoid double counting, and a country's own government is not counted as a donor.
      • Attention is measured two ways, both partial. Reports: the latest 20 per country from ReliefWeb's public feed, so the time they span gives a rate (reports per week). That counts what aid organisations publish, which is not the same as general news. News: a sample from GDELT, English plus each country's main language, from roughly the last week. Local news also comes from the public RSS feeds of about 58 outlets based in these countries (some refused automated access and were skipped), filtered for humanitarian keywords in their own language; state-run outlets are labelled.
      • "State-linked" means an outlet owned, funded or controlled by a government. That describes ownership, not accuracy, and the list is short, so the shares are a floor.
      • Propaganda cannot be measured from a headline count. Whether a specific article misleads needs reading it, so this page shows who is speaking and leaves that judgement to case studies.
      • Correlation is not cause: research on disaster relief finds that media attention can shape who gets help, but this page does not claim that attention causes a country's funding level.
      • Machine translation can be wrong. Original headlines are always shown; read the source when it matters.
      • Donation links are starting points, not endorsements or ratings. Check an independent charity evaluator before you give.
